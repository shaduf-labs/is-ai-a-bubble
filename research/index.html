What changed—and why it matters

The previous programme examined useful parts of the question but did not connect them into a whole assessment. Its four reports no longer serve as four equally current answers. They remain dated history and optional detail. Superseding their overall standing does not automatically discredit every finding inside them.

The earlier baseline contained contemporary company results and productivity studies, not just historical analogies. The restart did not discover useful applications or profitable suppliers for the first time. It connects and weighs the evidence more fully; it is not an account of a sudden reversal from fictitious activity to universal financial soundness.

Broader synthesis—not a newly discovered fact

From separate cases to a whole assessment

Previously: the Summary said real activity was visible but broad returns and valuations remained open, then directed readers through separate cash, financing and debt cases.

Now: the current assessment judges the expansion real but identifies concentrated speculative and financing vulnerabilities. It connects measured customer benefits, monetization, company funding, recovery scenarios and ownership-price requirements.

Why and consequence: the shortcoming was an incomplete overall explanation, not that every earlier caution or positive result was false. The new account weighs both the sustainable case and possible loss mechanisms without making readers assemble the answer themselves. Still unresolved: independently financed final demand, actual cohort returns and the scale of losses that could propagate.

Correction of a missed disclosure—already made in report 02

Amazon’s payment was not first established by this restart

Previously: report 01 used the February announcement and left Amazon’s $50bn OpenAI funding unverified. Report 02 then read the already-available June-quarter filing and explicitly acknowledged the avoidable omission.

Now, carrying that correction forward: Amazon reported $15bn invested in the first quarter, $13.7bn in the second and $21.3bn after June, before the July filing—$50bn in total. That is investor-reported funding, not a recipient-bank-account audit.

Why and consequence: the correction changed the pool’s knowledge, not the event date. It narrowed the payment uncertainty before the new programme. Still unresolved: the exact final payment day, recipient-side verification and whether customer receipts can support the resulting purchases. This payment does not establish every other investor’s installment.

The original correction in report 02.

Sources: Amazon June 2026 filing, investment note.

Changed comparison window—not an additional bill

The trailing year and the half-year overlap

Previously: report 02 and the Spending page compared January–June 2026 whole-parent cash flows.

Now: a common July 2025–June 2026 window adds context: $510.703bn of cash property-and-equipment purchases and $660.314bn of operating cash. The contained January–June window remains $294.800bn and $322.486bn, respectively.

Why and consequence: the longer comparison and shorter-window investment intensity sharpen the funding question; they do not contradict the earlier half-year figures. The windows are not added or subtracted as successive observations. Both cover whole companies, not AI alone. Still unresolved: AI-specific allocation and investment recovery. Operating cash minus gross purchases remains a limited subtraction, not company-defined free cash flow.

Exact inputs, definitions and original sources.

Later-period balance-sheet evidence—not comparable deleveraging

June replaces March for current analysis, not for history

Previously: report 04 presented CoreWeave’s 31 March principal of $25.149bn, with $11.718bn through 2027—46.6%. Later May and June financing events were separated, and the June-quarter source gap remained explicit.

Now: the June filing supplies $35.551bn principal, of which $10.597bn was scheduled through 2027—29.8%. It also reports August borrowing; September proposed financing is separately identified, not counted as verified proceeds.

Why and consequence: a later balance sheet and improved source coverage supersede the old snapshot for current analysis. The lower share is not a like-for-like fall in risk: the remaining calendar window is shorter, balances and financing changed, and the denominator grew. Still unresolved: a complete 18 September debt roll-forward, actual project cash and defensible collateral recoveries. June plus selected announcements is not a current balance.

Sources: CoreWeave June 2026 filing; CoreWeave 17 September financing proposal.

Later attributed monetization evidence—not audited cash profitability

The lab discussion cannot stay frozen at older loss narratives

Previously: the earlier pages documented scoped usefulness and profitable suppliers but did not incorporate the later Anthropic adjusted-profit reporting.

Now: Reuters’ August report attributes an annualized revenue rate above $65bn to a source, measured at end-July. The Financial Times reported in September that Anthropic expected a second consecutive quarter of adjusted operating profit, excluding stock compensation.

Why and consequence: this is favorable counterevidence to blanket inability-to-profit claims, not a claim that profitability was absent from every older page. Still unresolved: full investor materials and audited cash economics after serving, research and reinvestment. Research did not obtain the full paywalled FT article.

Sources: Reuters, 17 August: Anthropic revenue reporting; Financial Times: Anthropic adjusted-profit expectation.

Expanded analytical coverage—not established fair values

Recovery and valuation now have explicit assumptions

Previously: the Method page and reports left security valuation and whole-investment recovery for later work.

Now: the current assessment models a hypothetical investment cohort and the cash paths needed to support selected ownership values. It also distinguishes confidential submissions, public filing exhibits, reported IPO preparations and completed proceeds.

Why and consequence: these additions make financial expectations inspectable rather than assuming usefulness answers every money question. The old Method page’s coverage statement is historical. Still unresolved: actual cohort economics, private terms and a fully reproducible current public-market value. Required revenue is not a company target, forecast, measured shortfall or fair-value verdict.

Evidence boundary maintained—not a verified payment

Keep the narrower prepaid-forward characterization

Previously: report 03 described an executed $1.5bn NVIDIA prepaid-forward obligation without claiming verified receipt.

Now: the current reader account retains that boundary. Section 1(f) requires payment within three business days; the cited exhibit does not establish receipt. A separate $1.5bn subscription is conditional on a qualifying IPO.

Why and consequence: a later account should not be stronger than its evidence. An executed obligation is not proof of receipt, and the two contracts are not $3bn already paid. Still unresolved: payment and receipt evidence beyond this exhibit. This is not a finding of nonpayment.

Sources: NVIDIA prepaid-forward agreement, section 1(f); NVIDIA offering-linked subscription agreement.

Different forecast vintage—not a realized energy result

The power outlook retains its date and scope

Previously: report 01 explicitly retained the IEA’s April 2025 scenario of about 945 TWh of global data-center use in 2030.

Now: the current assessment uses the April 2026 outlook: an estimated 485 TWh in 2025 and a central projection of 950 TWh in 2030. Why and consequence: a later forecast vintage updates context; the 2030 figure remains a projection, not proof of delivered AI capacity or a failed earlier forecast. Still unresolved: powered, utilized capacity tied to investment cohorts and customer cash.

Sources: IEA April 2026 energy and AI outlook.
